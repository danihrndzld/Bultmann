Whether a man is really strong and free within is shown by the extent to which he feels that he is in a position to confess his own weaknesses and failings. When we are anxiously concerned to assert our merits, we take trouble to hide our weaknesses and to gloss over our faults. We easily succumb to bragging and lying, and especially to a dangerous trick of self-deception. There are many men who deceive themselves by refusing to face the fact of their weaknesses and faults, and flatter themselves by the splendid picture of their personalities which they conjure up. There floats before their minds an image of what they must be or would like to be, and they end by fancying that they are so in reality.
<|det|>text [52, 349, 874, 730]<|/det|>The Pharisee too is a man who deceives himself. There are many various ideals which infatuate the minds of men, for example the ideal of the quaintly interesting, or the heroic in various walks of life. The ideal which haunts the mind of the Pharisee is that of the severely moral and pious man. He belongs to those who do not shrink from energetic forms of self-sacrifice, and who do not spare themselves pain and trouble in order to obey the commandment, what the right and the good demands. And yet such people deceive themselves. All their moral exertion is vain, because they do not perform what is good from the motive of delight in the good, of serving others and increasing their happiness, but in order to be able to praise themselves. It is such men against whose blindness Jesus struggled; they are the “righteous who need no physician”—that is, who think that they need no physician and who rebuked Jesus as a “glutton and drunkard” because He did not associate Himself with their rigid moralism.
