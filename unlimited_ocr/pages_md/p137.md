And in so far as we see that we can be a source of blessing to others, we may justifiably feel that we are something of value in ourselves. It then becomes clear to us that it is not what we have planned and intentionally done which confers the richest content and meaning on our lives, but rather what we have gratefully received and faithfully preserved as a gift, and that the greatest of these gifts bestowed on us is love. We come to see that in the fellowship of love and trust, whose secret network mysteriously interpenetrates the world of the tangible and of purposeful organization, our true essential life is rooted.

<|det|>text [60, 400, 885, 895]<|/det|>"Behold, I stand at the door and knock." This finally applies to the word of Holy Scripture, which summons us to vigilance and readiness. This word is not faded or remote; old as it is, it speaks to us ever anew. It strikes home to the heart of our daily life as the word which sounds from another world. It vibrates essentially in the very sound of our church bells, so different from all the other sounds which beat upon our ears—an alien mysterious sound at the heart of our so familiar world. And it would be a sad day for us if ever the music of church bells were to become silent in our villages and towns. For it is this music which calls us away from the world of visible things, the world of our scheming and talking, inviting us to gather ourselves together to hear the word of God which resounds from the invisible world of eternity: "Behold, I stand at the door and knock; if any one hears my voice and opens the door, I will come in to him and eat with him, and he with me." Who is this "I" which calls us thus? It is eternity—the eternity which addresses us also in the messengers it sends.
