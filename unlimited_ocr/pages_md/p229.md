<|det|>text [80, 68, 905, 246]<|/det|>Does not this, however, imply that we must be ready to renounce all hope? To lay as it were in sacrifice at the feet of God all our hopes that our lot may be transformed? Yes, it does in fact mean that. And is not this the same thing as is suggested in Dante's poem by the inscription which is seen over the gate leading to hell: "Abandon all hope, ye who enter here!" Is then the way to God the way to hopelessness and hell?
<|det|>text [80, 249, 905, 361]<|/det|>Yes and no. The way to God leads not to hell but through hell, or, in Christian terms, through the cross. It leads us not to hopelessness but to a hope which transcends all human hope; and we must silence all human hope, if that divine hope is to dawn for us.
<|det|>text [84, 362, 912, 769]<|/det|>We must make this clear to ourselves: for man as he is, laden with wishes and plans, with longings and hope—and this means for us all, we who form our dream pictures as to how our life should go according to our desire and will—for all of us the way to God is the way into that darkness which for us means hell. There are moments in our life when from our human point of view there seems no difference between God and the devil, when God, quite incomprehensibly to us, destroys our dearest and loveliest; and not only that which forms the goal of our desires, and brings the greatest happiness into our lives, but often too those things of which we think we may say that they have been initiated and built up by the aid of His spirit, and in the spirit of the good and noble. The breath of the Lord can sweep away our all in a second and for our eyes there is nothing left but a comfortless waste. That is the meaning of God: His majesty annihilates whatever stands independently. His word is a word that slays.
