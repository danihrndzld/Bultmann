<|det|>text [96, 183, 927, 635]<|/det|>Sunday should lead us to meditate on this, among other things: what is the ultimate meaning of all our busy care and toil? Of this bustling activity in which we all seem to be endlessly engaged? It comes to an end of course with death, and what final significance has our work in view of this end to which we are moving? Have we become more mature, more prepared for eternity? Have we won an inner peace of soul? Can we thank God as we look back on this past week? Have we become more cheerful and kind? Or are we only tired and vexed? Or do we find it uncomfortable and repugnant to examine ourselves at all? Are we seeking in the midst of a feverish preoccupation with our work to flee from ourselves because we know how poor and empty we are? Yes, Sunday is intended to remind us of the fact that we are truly weary and heavy laden however much we are inclined to forget it. Its purpose is to draw our attention to those hidden things of the spirit which we most sorely need. "The kingdom of God is not eating and drinking", says Paul (neither is it toil and care), "but righteousness and peace and joy in the Holy Ghost."
<|det|>text [96, 636, 930, 910]<|/det|>“Come to me!” thus the peal of church bells on Sunday invites us. They call us to enter the house of God, and to surrender ourselves to the sway of God’s word.
