But Luther's dictum remains true: "If God's word and sign is not there and is not recognized, then it avails nothing even though God Himself were there!" No, nature is not the sphere of God's self-revelation. Nevertheless, Scripture declares that God is effectually present in nature. "The heavens are telling the glory of God and the firmament proclaims His handiwork. Day to day pours forth speech, and night to night declares knowledge. There is no speech nor are there words; their voice is not heard" (Ps. 19: 2-4). He, the Omnipotent and Omnipresent, is also present in nature. We to whom He speaks in the word of Scripture and the gospel of Jesus Christ are not intended to come to know Him first from the world of nature. But as those who already know Him through Jesus Christ, we are intended to look for the witness to His presence which is implicit in nature, and to praise and worship Him with grateful hearts for the traces of His being which we see there. Hence we do not speak of God's self-disclosure in nature but we do speak of God's presence in nature. The question is: in what way do we encounter this presence?

<|det|>text [92, 585, 920, 907]<|/det|>It is unquestionable that the glad and grateful appreciation of the beauty and vitality of nature is not peculiar to Christians, but is characteristic of all men who are not inwardly consumed by care and grief. It is unquestionable too that this joy in nature is not a merely aesthetic attitude. Such joy is essentially different from the joy we feel in the contemplation of a beautiful picture or a great work of art. Nature does not invite us to merely recreative or cultural attitudes. The feeling with which we walk through the fields and woods in spring and delight in their deepening green, or with which the town-dweller cultivates his little garden plot and rejoices to see its growth, is a feeling which has a quite characteristic tone.
