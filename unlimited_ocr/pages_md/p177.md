Just that of God's sovereign freedom. God is free over against all human claims! "I choose to give to this last as I give to you. Am I not allowed to do what I choose with what belongs to me?" says the householder in the parable, and the remark is intended to illustrate God's absolute freedom. Man must bow before the transcendence of this divine freedom and not ask why. God is sovereignty free and unfettered by human claims; and whoever dares to speak of God or to reckon with God must be clear about this. Man is subject to the free will of God and has no right to make claims against God.

<|det|>text [67, 396, 890, 825]<|/det|>Thus far our parable teaches us nothing other than the parable about the master and his slaves. But it does not teach us this lesson alone; there is something decisively important which it adds. What is the real sting of the offence which the grumbling workmen feel? They are not of course offended because they have been badly treated, because their pay has been reduced or kept back. The sting lies rather in the fact that the others who have worked less than themselves receive none the less the same reward. And on what grounds does it rest that the employer gives these latter the same recompense? On the grounds of his sheer kindness and generosity. He shames the grumblers by his question: "Do you begrudge my generosity?" Did not they whom no one had hired need a day's wages just as much as they who were first hired? Were they not also men who suffered hunger and thirst? Did they not likewise want money for the needs of their life and of their families? Were they to be made to suffer because up to evening they had found no remunerative work?
