It is unquestionable, and particularly so for Christians, that the sensitive feeling for the splendours and the abundant vitalities of nature is a feeling which conceals the sense of God's presence and working, that the astonished delight in the secret powers of nature really expresses our wonder at the power and glory of God, and that the thankful exultation in nature which rings out in poetry and song is at bottom praise of God the Creator. Hence it is not surprising that when such joy and gratitude are expressed in poetry, the poem also speaks of God. So it is in the austere Pilgrim's Song of Eichendorff: "When God wishes to bestow on you great favour" . . . and concluding thus: "I accept in simple faith the wise ruling of the good God; He who wills to maintain the streams, the larks, the woods and the fields, has also guided my life for the best." So it is too in the spring song of Goethe, Ganymede, which to many of us is so attractive in the musical setting of Schubert. The poem is penetrated with love and yearning directed to the "all-loving Father":
<|det|>text [65, 447, 885, 557]<|/det|>"Beloved spring, how you encircle me with your flame in the bright radiance of morning. O infinite beauty, with the manifold raptures of love the holy feeling of your everlasting warmth and glow pierces my heart." And so it goes on to the conclusion:
<|det|>text [65, 559, 885, 650]<|/det|>"Upwards, upwards soars my heart, while the hovering clouds sink down to meet the yearnings of love. To me they incline! Upwards I rise to their bosom! I return the embrace of love! Upwards I soar to your breast, O all-loving Father!"
