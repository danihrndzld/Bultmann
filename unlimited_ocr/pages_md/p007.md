Man who feels himself surrounded thus by the mysterious and the uncanny, who is not secure of himself, who is aware that his life is both driven onward and thwarted by forces which are more powerful than himself, lives in anxiety; and in his anxiety he worships these transcendent forces as gods. And even when finally he concludes that the various powers are one and the same, he is still not freed from this anxiety; for he still does not know the one God who is Lord of heaven and earth.
<|det|>text [43, 284, 861, 466]<|/det|>The more gods or the more altars there are, so many more the symbols of human fear. The altar consecrated “to an unknown god” was also such a symbol. For such altars (and they bear too the inscription: “To the god whom it concerns”) arise from this enveloping fear: here is a god at work whom I do not yet know and whose name I do not yet know! Hence he must have an altar at which he may be so to speak anonymously worshipped.

The second factor emerges from the words of Paul: "The God who made the world and everything in it . . . does not live in shrines made by man" (v. 24ff.), "and we ought not to think that the Deity is like gold or silver or stone, a representation by the art and imagination of man" (v. 29).
