<|det|>text [112, 194, 941, 442]<|/det|>Let us look once more at the disposition of these grumblers in the parable. If anyone perchance asks: did they experience God's goodness, did it not rather come to the men who were hired last? the very question betrays a wrongness of outlook. Is it not always a great and splendid thing to see God's goodness in action? Should we not be ready to rejoice whenever we see a manifestation of God's generosity, whether it be I myself or someone else who is its object? Yes, that is just the point; are we able to feel joy in the good which comes to others? For of course this capacity to rejoice with others is the sign of a noble and kind disposition.
<|det|>text [112, 443, 941, 848]<|/det|>"Do you begrudge my generosity?" asks the householder in the parable. Should not the surly fellow have been ashamed as he realized that he ought to have been pleased to think that his comrades were being so generously treated? Instead of this his heart was filled with a petty envy when he saw that another man was getting better treatment than himself. And do we suppose that a heart like this could be capable of perceiving God's sheer goodness when he himself becomes the object of it? No! such a man will never be aware of it, even when generous treatment is meted out to him, for he will not be capable of confessing with shame: "Lord, I am not in the least worthy of all the faithfulness and mercy which Thou hast shown to Thy servant!" He will rather take the gift as though he merited it. Still more will such a man be incapable of perceiving God's kindness in the midst of adversity and sorrow. The awareness of God's kindness to us implies that we ourselves are kind and generous in disposition.
