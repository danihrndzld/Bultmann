<|det|>text [96, 150, 922, 262]<|/det|>If then Jesus is He who is to come, and if, after His coming, the world is renewed for the man who allows his being to be transformed by the Saviour, then we have also the answer to our other question: how can we, after He has come, still speak of Him as the One who is to come?
<|det|>text [96, 263, 922, 534]<|/det|>Just because through His coming, our outlook on the world and on time is completely changed. The event of His coming is not an event within the process of world history, an event such as is straightway merged with the past; it is rather an event which signifies the end of the process of world history. Those who believe in the One who is to come are lifted out of the flux of time into the transcendence of eternity; they have truth, purity, and fullness of life; as pain and death have no further power to reduce them to despair, so the evil around them and within them can no longer make them afraid. It is engulfed in the full tide of divine grace.
<|det|>text [96, 535, 922, 852]<|/det|>Faith gives to the Christian this unshakable certitude. And it is the characteristic situation of Christians that so long as they remain on earth they must walk by faith, not sight. They who in Christ are made victorious over the world of time, nevertheless still remain immersed in that world. And just because the coming of Jesus is not merely an event taking its place in past history, but an event which spells the end of all history, for them the One who has come is always at the same time the One who is to come and will be so to the end of the world and of time. For ourselves who still are implicated in the process of world history, He is always the One who is to come, always the One who ever anew enables us to transcend the flux and vicissitudes of time, with its sorrow and sin and death.
