To refuse to believe on Jesus does not mean to reject as incredible some curious doctrines about Him, but to refuse to allow His validity as God’s challenge to the world, to close one’s eyes to the intrinsic finiteness of earthly things, to consider the world the final authority both in what it claims and in what it offers, to shut oneself off from the grace which flows from the holiness of God and is readily available for this world as soon as it confesses that it needs divine grace. This is sin.
<|det|>text [65, 337, 893, 857]<|/det|>Again, the Spirit convinces the world of the true meaning of righteousness. The world too speaks of righteousness. According to its own criteria it makes judgments, pronounces one man to be right, another to be wrong, honours one and defames another. And it makes especial use of the principle that the course of history discloses judgment in that it declares: in the successes and achievements of history, the right is manifested. Right is what triumphs in the world. In fact, the right and success are one and the same thing; it supposes that, in the language of the gospel, righteousness means the same thing as ascendancy and victory. But the criteria of what may be called victory differ. The judgment of the world presupposes that victory must be revealed in what is visible, and failure in the visible sphere spells defeat and destruction. But the Spirit teaches us thus: “Of righteousness because I go to the Father and you will see me no more.” This is very remarkable: Jesus’ failure in the visible sphere, His death and His departure from the world is His victory! He has overcome the world, and His death is the very confirmation and seal of His work which was to confront the world with God’s challenge to it. Had he belonged to the world, then the world, which loves its own (John 15: 19) would gladly have welcomed Him.
