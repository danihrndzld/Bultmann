Well, has he not such a right? After all he is not responsible for having entered the stream of life in this world, and he therefore seems to be under no obligation of gratitude to anyone for the fact of his existence, and to bear no responsibility for what he proposes to do with his life. Yet he has claims on life, he voices demands. And this is the fundamental sin of mankind—the failure to recognize that man is a creature, man's assertion of his right to live.
<|det|>text [111, 291, 934, 740]<|/det|>As a creature he should be grateful for his life to his Creator. But how can he be, when his life is one long wearisome struggle with adverse fate? How can he give thanks when his life has become a burden to him, an unendurable torment? When he sees no meaning or sense in his life, and the meaning which he himself would like to confer on his life is destroyed by a ruthless fate? Is it not understandable that there have been men who have cursed the day of their birth? That there have been men who have extolled as the supreme bliss never to have been born, and as the second highest bliss to die young? Certainly it is understandable that a man should love life because he feels in himself the energy and the joy to accomplish work and taste of pleasure, or when his life, however steep the pathway, is leading to success and fulfilment. But what of the man whose powers are impaired or destroyed, who because of illness or poverty must forbid himself every enjoyment? How can a man be grateful for the gift of life when his whole being “is stretched upon the rack” and bent to work which is weakening to the body and paralysing to the mind?
