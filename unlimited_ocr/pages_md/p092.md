<|det|>text [102, 92, 930, 567]<|/det|>Just as none of us, even those who are most disappointed, should look back without gratitude, so none should fail to ask himself the searching question, “Where have I failed? what has remained undone?” And if he faces the test of this inner self-examination, if he discovers where he has been neglectful, and what should be improved in the future—and which of us must needs not confess so much?—is it really enough to make up one’s mind to do better in the future? There is no doubt that at the end of next term we shall again have to look back in shame on the things we have failed to do and the points at which there is room for improvement, and so on throughout the entire course of our lives. We should have to have a great deal of time at our disposal if we were really to make up for what we have neglected, and the truth is that we have no such extra time to dispose of. Each future to which we look forward brings its own tasks, and what is gone, is gone for ever. Our lives are inescapably caught up in the ever-flowing time stream. Who knows how soon he will have used up this available time? We do not lay hold of peace by our repentance and good intentions; we remain in perpetual dispeace and restlessness.
<|det|>text [106, 568, 934, 817]<|/det|>This state of affairs suggests that our repentance has not the power to make us better. We always remain the same; we are not masters of ourselves. If it is the case that a retrospective glance such as we are taking to-day always shows that our consciousness includes the twofold feeling—grateful joy and tormenting shame—then this is no incidental thing but rather is suggestive of the very pattern and structure of our being. This characteristic is the fissure that runs through it. The really torturing and painful thing is not the particular actions in which we have erred or which we have failed to perform, but just this: the inescapable dualism in our nature.
