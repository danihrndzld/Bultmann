<|det|>text [65, 42, 895, 384]<|/det|>The phrase “unworthy servants” means the same as “wretched”; it does not mean servants who do their duty badly so that when we have only half fulfilled or not fulfilled at all our obligations, God is a terrifying master for us. The parable is not speaking for example of God as our Judge, before whom we stand as imperfect, impure, and sinful men. It is speaking about God as Lord, before whom we stand forth as miserable creatures, even when we have done all that was commanded us. In that this is so, it speaks a message of comfort just to those who suffer from the consciousness of their imperfection. God does not consider whether you are more or less perfect! In His sight no one is better than another, if perfection is the standard: “When you have done all that is commanded you, say, ‘We are unworthy servants!’” This is what Luther means when he sings:

<|det|>text [65, 550, 893, 821]<|/det|>Have we now some idea what our parable wishes to teach us, and in what way it is a message of divine grace? It is a comforting word of grace because it teaches us that we wretched human beings cannot live otherwise than by the inspiration of God's grace. It teaches us this, precisely because it shows us God as He really is, as our Lord, before whom we poor mortals can have no right, can raise no claim. It is a liberating and saving word because it frees us from the illusion that we might, because of what we are and achieve, lay claim to some justification in the sight of God. Only he who surrenders all presumption to right and claim before God, only he can live by God's grace.
