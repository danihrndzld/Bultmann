<|det|>text [106, 50, 926, 388]<|/det|>We can hardly fail to think of our nation as a whole. To think of how long ago it first heard God's call and invitation when the gospel was first preached to our forefathers, the old Germanic tribes; and how again it was struck by the call of God when in the sixteenth century the preaching of Luther caused the original message of the gospel to ring out anew. We cannot help thinking of the days when Germany was a country dominated by the Church. Is not this shown by the churches which belong to the whole picture of our villages, by the minsters and cathedrals which tower up in our towns and cities? Is it not shown by the Christian teaching which moulded the minds of our youth in school and at church? Is it not shown by church customs and festivals which were interwoven in the very texture of our nation's life, by holy baptism and holy marriage and church burial?
<|det|>text [106, 390, 926, 795]<|/det|>Our churches still belong essentially to the picture presented by our towns and villages; and still on Sundays peals of bells ring out over houses and meadows. I do not propose to enlarge on the fact that already there are villages the focal point of whose life is no longer the church. But I must ask: how many churches are filled Sunday by Sunday with devout hearers of the Word? How many of our compatriots still allow themselves to be summoned by the bells to enter the house of God? For how many are the great cathedrals of our cities no longer the house of God where the word of God speaks to them and finds them, but simply artistic monuments, an enduring product of the German mind and civilization? What has happened to the Christian education of youth? How many people still feel themselves seriously bound by church discipline and obligation? We all know that Germany is no longer a Christian country; that church life is now only a surviving remnant, and that many desire and hope that even this remnant will shortly disappear.
