They are of this world. Their radiance fades in the very acute distress which now grips our hearts; they fail us in times of trouble, bitter sorrow and sore temptation. And we surmise that the “light of life” is something which lies beyond them, just as indescribable and vague as that which hovers before the minds of all of us and which the naïve man calls “luck”; no one can precisely say what it is. Or again just as indescribable and vague as what we mean when we speak of the fulfilment and fruition of our being, of what we essentially are or what we wish to be and ought to be and yet never are and of which we have only the vaguest intuition! But is it not just that which Jesus means when He promises us the light of life? He presupposes that everyone longs for it. And the newness of His message is only this: “I am it!” “I am the light of life!”
<|det|>text [111, 453, 934, 877]<|/det|>And now what this “light of life” is may be formulated somewhat more plainly, in so far as we may express more clearly what it means for our lives, however mysterious it may be. Why is it that this thing which we long for, without knowing clearly its nature, is called the “light of life”? Light is the power of illumination bringing clarity of vision so that in consequence we walk “in the light” and do not grope in darkness, uncertain of the way. Hence the “light of life” would mean that life for us gains transparent clarity, that the oppressive problems, the torturing mysteries of our life come to an end; that we are no longer afraid of what is to come, of what threatens, that we are no longer strangers to ourselves, that, so to speak, everything has become self-explanatory. “In that day you will ask me no questions,” says Jesus to His disciples when He promises them the joy which no man can take from them (John 16: 23). No longer to need to ask questions because our whole existence has become full of light—that is what it means to have the “light of life”.
