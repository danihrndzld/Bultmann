The man who always supposes that the things which are given him are only his just due, who takes the good that comes his way as though he had a right to it, fails to perceive God's overflowing generosity, and misses the best in life, the sentiments of joy and gratitude and contentment. For such a man is of course never satisfied, and always thinks that his claim exceeds what is actually given him. God's goodness implies His freedom; and as His goodness is inexhaustible, exceeding all the goodness of man, so also is His freedom unlimited, transcending all human freedom. Hence if we are to experience God's goodness we must be prepared also to acknowledge His freedom, and to silence all claimfulness. For this reason Jesus in His parable emphasizes God's freedom so sharply as to arouse at first a shock of offence in His hearer: "Am I not allowed to do what I choose with what belongs to me?"
<|det|>text [58, 397, 881, 779]<|/det|>Now of course when we speak in these terms of God's goodness and freedom many tormenting questions arise. Is God's goodness in reality experienced by all men? Is it always only their own fault if they fail to perceive how the gracious goodness of God is active in their own lives? For example, did the labourers who were hired first experience the kindness of their master? There are certainly many sad and tragic destinies in which it is not easy to trace any manifestation of God's kindness, and indeed there is much human distress in face of which heart-searching questions spring to our minds. Even to those who ask these questions the words of Jesus contain a message of comfort; but our parable does not examine such questions directly. It proposes to teach us only one thing, namely, the unity of God's goodness and freedom. And we should not allow our attention to be diverted from its main purpose by other questions which receive an answer elsewhere.
