Prayerful waiting upon God bears fruit in man's inner detachment and freedom, with the kindness and honesty which flow from it; in man's peaceableness of disposition and readiness to suffer for the good. And now conversely it should be said that all this is only possible on the basis of genuine trust. <|det|>text [75, 285, 896, 508]<|/det|>The one who waits! Thus may we characterize in general terms the life of the Christian; the description fits him just as does that which qualifies him as the viator, the pilgrim. In this connexion the thought of Jung-Stilling occurs to our minds: “Blessed are the homesick; for they shall return to their homes!” The Christian is essentially a pilgrim who is engaged in a journey, who has turned his back on the old world and is directing his steps to the future which God will create. And for the same reason we may describe him as the one who waits.
<|det|>text [75, 511, 896, 849]<|/det|>But is not this attitude characteristic of all men as such? Is not every man at heart filled with discontent about the present and expectation of the future? Is it not part of man's essential being never to be able to say to the present moment: "Tarry, I pray, thou art so beautiful!" Is it not essentially human to be ever occupied with a tense expectation of the coming moment that shall bring us satisfaction? No doubt we often deceive ourselves about this, and many a time it may seem to us that we experience a moment of fulfilment to which we would like to hold fast. But if such a feeling does not rest simply on the suppression and temporary oblivion of our cares, which will soon assail us again, then in a really fulfilled moment it is only apparently so. For such a moment always contains the promise of a future, and fills us precisely with tense expectancy.
