Understood on these lines, the beatitude would have the same meaning as the words of Goethe: “Him who ever manfully toils, we can redeem.” In the Biblical context, however, righteousness has a different sense; those are praised as blessed whom God justifies, whom God acknowledges, whom God accredits. The promise is addressed to those who suffer not merely from the frailty and pain of human life in general, but from their own sense of inferiority and unworthiness. The reference is to those who are oppressed by the feeling that they never can wholly be what they would like to be, that neither before others nor before God can they appear and present themselves as what in their deepest being they truly are, that their essential being is always neutralized by weakness and guilt, that they can never give themselves as they really intend, and so always suffer from the feeling of being misunderstood. In fact is there not in all of us this yearning desire to be able to give clear and effective expression to our essential being and our deepest will, to stand forth in the sight of all and in God’s sight as we feel that we might be? Yes, this desire is latent in all men. Yet often it is suppressed or distorted into a tense and nervous striving to bring one’s merit to light, to thrust oneself forward by achievement or pose, by a convulsive effort to make an impression on others, and in that impression to find a mirror of one’s true self.
<|det|>text [108, 597, 937, 891]<|/det|>Oh that we were freed from this urge! That we could express ourselves spontaneously as we are! That our being could unfold itself effectively and purely, and give a true reflection of itself in the eyes of all! No doubt there is also an attitude of resigned abandonment of effort, of contempt for human judgments in general, the withdrawal of man into himself in proud self-isolation from his fellows. And this attitude may well suggest a certain strength of soul, and compel our respect. But the beatitude is not meant for it.
