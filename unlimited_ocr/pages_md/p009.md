In the dark primaeval forest in which man as it were first finds himself, he begins to adapt things to his basic needs; he introduces light and order into nature whose powers he observes and subdues to his own purposes. The circle widens itself more and more until it at last becomes so extensive that the darkness of the world recedes into remotest distance and man loses sight of the element of mystery. Thus he has no longer any reason to erect an altar to the “unknown God”. But has he been able at the same time to efface the fear of life which lay behind the belief in the “unknown God”? Or does it only lurk secretly at the heart of his life, stilled by his busy activities or hidden by the veil of a stupendous illusion, only to cry out more loudly at times? Has then man really become master of his world and his life? Have the world and existence in truth lost their uncanny and mysterious elements in consequence of the powerful organization of human culture and civilization? And do there not still remain, in the inner life of man, mysterious forces which as it were are only waiting for the moment when they can spring forth with daemonic energy?
<|det|>text [44, 558, 866, 830]<|/det|>When Paul says: “What therefore you worship as unknown, this I proclaim to you”, he is admitting that in the cultic worship springing from anxiety an element of truth is contained. Such a cult implies the search for the one true God, because it implies the awareness that man is not his own master. Man will never be freed from anxiety by seeking to illuminate the world, by seeking to control and organize it in his own interests, but only by facing the question which his anxiety suggests, by giving due recognition to his religious awareness, so that it may ultimately lead him to the knowledge of the one God who is Lord of heaven and earth.
