If a man still reckons with God he strives to gain God's approval; but he does this in a false way since he views God as a man on whom he can make an impression by the count of his good deeds, just as the Pharisee does who boasts of his merits before God. Hence in truth men seek to stand in favour with God and at the same time they flee from God because they suppose that they can obtain the credit they desire by their own efforts and performances.
<|det|>text [115, 224, 936, 583]<|/det|>But how then are they to find it? How are they to be liberated from the tense struggle to win favour and prestige? How shall they gain freedom and security of life, and the assured conviction that their lives are not worthless but genuinely good? In no other way but by fleeing from themselves and seeking refuge in God! And it is just this which the portrait of the publican is intended to teach us. He “stood afar off and would not even lift up his eyes to heaven, but beat his breast saying, ‘God, be merciful to me a sinner’”. This man does not speak at great length. He does not compare himself with others and does not say for example: “Before Thee O God I lament the fact that I am not like other people who are so much better than I.” He does not enumerate his faults: “Here and there I have forgotten myself! In one or two matters I have been unfaithful!” He says simply: “God be merciful to me a sinner!”
<|det|>text [115, 584, 936, 899]<|/det|>True self-knowledge in the face of God does not arise from comparing oneself with those who are better than oneself; nor from reflecting on what we have done badly and falsely. We may of course have plenty of occasions for self-examination and for remembering what we have done. And we must not silence the voice of conscience with its spontaneous warnings. But the important thing is not that in our prayers we should count up our sins before God. Just by doing so a secret feeling of pride may arise in our hearts as we reflect that at least our penitence is a work pleasing to God. And if we compare ourselves with others who are better than we, in that too the impulse to self-assertion may be concealed: we would like to be as others are and to feel distinguished and important.
