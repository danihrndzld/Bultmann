<|det|>text [69, 152, 895, 695]<|/det|>But of course what is necessary and what is sinful are so closely interwoven in human history that the skein can no longer be untangled. We are all of us immersed in a complex structure of life through which the power of evil winds in multifarious ways, whether we like it or not, and the individual cannot free himself from the muddle. He can of course make clear to himself the trend of things, and he ought not to look on with indifference at the destructive process which is violating nature; he must attempt to restore a due respect for nature, and he may be able to do his part in helping to ensure that the process of ruthless exploitation is limited to what is strictly essential. But our text does not draw our attention to the picture of nature in order to inculcate in us the right attitude towards it, but simply in order to open our eyes to the real situation with which we are confronted; nature involved against its will in the history of man and therefore subjected to the curse of ravage and destruction. We notice what she might and ought to be and what she is ever less and less. As we grow aware of these things we feel a sense of horror creeping over us, and we gain some surmise of the tragic doom which weighs upon our world, of the extent to which our life has become entangled in evil, and there arises in us a longing and a sighing for release and redemption.
