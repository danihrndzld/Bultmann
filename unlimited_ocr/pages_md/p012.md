<|det|>text [106, 229, 939, 501]<|/det|>Which of us to-day would think of growing angry when in a given case the approach of death was studiously concealed from a sick person? And who would take it upon himself to tell an unsuspecting person that he must soon die? But this only shows what we have at length come to, and how carefully we have concealed from ourselves the fundamental insecurity of our life; how little we take seriously the idea: "in Him we live and move and have our being". For the Lord of heaven and earth is also the Lord of life and death: and the thought "in Him we live and move and have our being" implies also media in vita in morte sumus (in the midst of life we are in death).
<|det|>text [106, 503, 939, 913]<|/det|>Thus the resources of technics do not suffice to make man master of death, nor of God. In fact he does not even thereby become master of himself, but only falls into the danger of losing his own. One of the means by which technics has influenced the human mind so seductively is its power of increasingly overcoming the boundaries of space and time, and thus of making the original limits, which reminded man forcibly of his finitude, shrink more and more, or at least appear to shrink; for the fact is that man never escapes these limits. But the less he feels them, so much the more he forgets all about them and forgets at the same time his finitude; so much the more riotously self-secure become his plans and calculations. In proportion as this happens, the hurry and fret of his life grow and the less time remains to him for consideration and reflection. Modern man who is swept away by the rush of life, which modern technology governs, only attains an acute awareness of time in those periods when his time is not filled by any specific content.
