<|det|>text [63, 108, 885, 399]<|/det|>Perhaps in these days we understand better than formerly the longing which the early Christians felt for “that day”, the day spelling the end of time, the “dear last day” as Luther used to call it. Of this day Jesus says: “In that day you will ask me no questions”, by which He means that then all tormenting questions will be silenced, all oppressive problems solved. In fact it is the very essence of joy that, when it envelops our being, questioning ceases; all becomes clear for us, self-explanatory. In an exposition of the words of Jesus about the lilies of the field and the birds of the air, Kierkegaard describes the latter as the great “teachers of joy” for the simple reason that they ask no questions and know no cares.
<|det|>text [63, 402, 885, 740]<|/det|>And is it suggested that this day, the “day” on which we shall no more ask questions, is to become for us a present reality? Is that joy which no one can take from us destined to be ours, we people who—especially just now—are filled with questioning and anxious care and every sort of distress? To be sure, gleams of joy and sunshine are granted us now and then. But we know too, as Hölderlin says: “Joy flashes across our path from time to time, but it does not properly belong to mortal beings.” And we also understand the cry of the poet who in the moment of fulfilled joy implores death quickly to snatch him away: “Now am I blessed! pierce my heart now!” (C. F. Meyer); for the poet knows that the moment of joy will vanish, it will fade into the grey light of common day, life will once more be filled with questioning and anxious care.
