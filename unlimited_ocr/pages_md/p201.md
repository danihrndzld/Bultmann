Or similarly in speaking to your friend: To-morrow either you or I will have vanished, perhaps, and we may never see each other again?” Certainly by this awareness we can train ourselves to survey as from a distance whatever happens to us, to remain in our deepest being unmoved by any human tragedy, so that when fate strikes us we are impervious and invulnerable to its blows. But these stoically-minded people are not they who mourn and whom Jesus extols as blessed. The fact is that they do not take upon themselves the burden of pain and sorrow; rather they flee from it by making themselves insensible and impassive. No, those are blessed who willingly embrace sorrow, who bear pain; who are not affrighted as if something most strange and offensive had befallen them, but who realize that in their pain they are sharing in the unfathomable pain which pierces all humanity to its depths, and who by such realization are freed to direct their gaze to the future; who wait for God’s consolation. “God will wipe away every tear from their eyes, and death shall be no more, neither shall there be mourning nor crying nor pain any more, for the former things have passed away” (Rev. 21: 4). How this will come to pass, we can have no idea; but the very renunciation of understanding is an essential part of the attitude of waiting.

The subsequent verse, however, does speak of the longings of humanity: “Blessed are those who hunger and thirst for righteousness, for they shall be satisfied.”
