<|det|>text [66, 176, 890, 355]<|/det|>And yet this thought contains a deep truth. Certainly, nature, regarded in itself, does not seem to be burdened with the curse of transience; but it does not exist for its own sake, for the drama of man's history is enacted in its midst, and whether it wills or no, it is drawn into the complex of that drama, becomes involved in the fate of man, is violated and despoiled by man. Do we not see this to-day, sometimes with a feeling of horror?
<|det|>text [66, 357, 890, 626]<|/det|>It is difficult to say where this process of violation begins. Perhaps already in the work of the tiller of the soil? In the cultivation of garden and forest? This would not seem to be the case. It is doubtful if Paul is thinking of this, for in such dealings with nature man remains as it were in conformity with the laws of nature's life; he tends its growth and reaps its crops for the maintenance of his life and obviously is not yet acting contrary to nature. For in so far as he needs nourishment and seeks his nourishment in the realm of nature, he is himself a being integral to nature, no other than the animal, and with seed time and harvest his life is merged in the great process of nature's vitalities.
<|det|>text [68, 628, 893, 898]<|/det|>But where does the violation of nature begin? We do not know at what point specifically Paul himself saw man's forcible subjugation of nature. He might have been thinking of all kinds of phenomena of his time; but he gives us no precise details. But there is no need to ponder the matter. The fact of man's violation of nature is plain, and it is plainest where human life has become most removed from the sphere of nature, in big cities and wherever technics have gained an all-controlling ascendancy. The very longing of the city dweller to take refuge in nature from time to time is an indication that in his normal environment nature has been driven out, or trampled on and killed.
