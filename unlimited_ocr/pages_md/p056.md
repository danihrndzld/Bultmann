The theme is the forsakenness of this little flock in the world, and, in conjunction with that, its superiority to the world. For in truth the evangelist does not propose to depict a farewell scene in a moving and poetical way, but in the situation of the disciples he is symbolizing the situation of the Christian fellowship as a whole. Their fate is ours; the promise made to them is likewise made to us.
<|det|>text [106, 316, 933, 857]<|/det|>The picture given of the disciples being left by Jesus to face, alone, a hostile world is not a picture of some transient episode but of the situation of the Christian community in the world at all times. Nor is it a scene which recurs only occasionally in periods of special crisis and stress, but it expresses the essential character of the universal Christian situation: namely, its abandonment in the great world. Why does this feature belong to the very essence of its life? The context of the passage we have chosen as our text makes this quite plain: believers in Him are the friends of Jesus whom He has chosen out of the world (15: 14ff.) and who belong to Him. This tie with Jesus means also however an indissoluble connexion with His fate. It is a link of a different kind from that which binds disciples to a great teacher or leader of humanity who brings to birth new insights and new ideals and initiates new tasks and programmes. Of course in this latter case too the disciples' association with their leader can involve a sharing of his fate, a participation not only in his ideals and aspirations, but also in his struggles, sacrifices and sufferings. Yet this is only true in a partial sense and usually only at the beginning of the movement when it is necessary to struggle for an acceptance of the new ideas in the world. Discipleship to Jesus, on the other hand, means necessarily and always a real sharing of His fate.
