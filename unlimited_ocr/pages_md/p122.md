The quality of the work done will suffer if a man thinks less of the matter itself than of the impression which he wishes to create on other people. We need hardly be reminded that there are men who from the urge to gain recognition undertake senseless and mad things, with the sole object of increasing their own sense of merit; just as there are children who, being incapable of doing anything useful, will do stupid and impudent things merely to draw attention to themselves.
<|det|>text [122, 260, 954, 849]<|/det|>Again, this lack of sincerity and objectivity goes hand in hand with a spirit of lovelessness. Men who are hungry for recognition may indeed compel respect and admiration for their accomplishments, but not love. Because they are concerned to win admiration from others, they in fact isolate themselves from others and make genuine friendship and fellowship impossible. When they take other people into account they do so in order to compare themselves with their fellows and to measure themselves against them with the object of strengthening the feeling of their own superior worth. Such a man becomes envious when he sees that others can do better than himself; he becomes arrogant and despises those to whom he thinks himself, in virtue of his achievements, superior. It is clear that true human society is ruined by this spirit. For community life flourishes only when the one freely appreciates the merits of the other and does not attempt to assert his own superiority, when each rejoices in the good which he sees in other people, gladly learns from them what can be learnt, gladly recognizes superior value wherever it is found. The man for whom other people serve only as a foil to set off his own sense of value becomes unjust, disputatious, harsh and unloving. He then resembles the portrait of the Pharisee who says: "I thank Thee, God, that I am not like other men!" We certainly feel that we should not like to have such people as friends. Do we wish to become such people ourselves?
