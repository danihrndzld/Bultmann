<|det|>text [115, 300, 936, 680]<|/det|>It might no doubt seem enticing to seek a position in life, the opportunity of which is provided by the Roman Catholic Church in its monastic system. There, man from the outset and once for all severs the connexions which bind him to the life of the world, so that nothing more can inwardly affect or wound him. Let us moreover be on our guard against too easily despising such an institution; for us Protestants it may always serve as a warning sign that we too as Christians are required to sever ourselves in spirit from the world, and to stand before God in naked solitariness. But we feel bound to add that this requirement is not truly fulfilled in monasticism. In monasticism ultimate freedom through self-surrender to God is not attained, because such freedom must prove itself in complete openness to the encounters of life. When man withdraws himself utterly from these encounters, he is betraying a secret fear of them and there is no fear in freedom.
<|det|>text [115, 684, 936, 908]<|/det|>If we wish to retain a complete openness towards the encounters of life, we shall of course also be ready for the pains and sorrows which are inseparable from all true sacrifice. But we shall be without fear, for we shall realize that pain and sorrow constantly lead us into the solitariness of confrontation by God where the miracle of genuine encounter with God occurs, where the certitude of the Easter faith rises triumphant; out of the darkness of death we pass into the light of life. And so in the midst of all pain a secret joy is characteristic of the Christian spirit.
