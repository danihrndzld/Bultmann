<|det|>text [139, 130, 967, 723]<|/det|>What day is meant by “that day”? The first verse of our text—certainly very familiar to many of us from listening to Brahms’ Requiem—runs: “So you have sorrow now, but I will see you again and your hearts will rejoice, and no one will take your joy from you.” These words were spoken before the event of Christ’s passion, and the day of reunion which Christ promises is Easter Day. But it is Easter Day in a quite special context of meaning. The phrase “on that day” occurs twice in our text and we must remember that this expression held a very special and secret meaning for primitive Christianity. The expression was used without further elucidation and its implication was “the day of the Lord”, i.e. the day of His return, the day on which this world would come to an end and a new heaven and earth would spring into being; the day on which would be fulfilled the promise: “Behold, I make all things new!” And the meaning of Jesus’ words is that the “day” which spells an end to this world has through Easter Day mysteriously become a present reality for believers. The believer through his faith to some extent anticipates the dawning of that “day” on which all things will be made new. How is this possible? The “day” when all creation will be renewed is anticipated by the Christian believer who is himself regenerated in his personal being, as Paul says: “Therefore if any one is in Christ, he is a new creation; the old has passed away, behold, the new has come” (2 Cor. 5: 17).

For such a one true prayer is already a reality. But the promise of the gift of prayer is preceded by the promise of the gift of joy, of a joy “which no one will take from us”.
