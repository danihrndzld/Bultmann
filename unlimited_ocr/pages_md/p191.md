We must come into the presence of God bared and naked, divested of all to which we would fain cling, stripped of the rags with which we would cover ourselves, so that we might win an understanding and consciousness of our true being. This is a terrible, stark loneliness frightening to the natural man; and the God who meets us in such loneliness may wear to us the appearance of the annihilating power of death. We must be ready to endure His consuming glance, and to be nothing in His sight. We must be prepared as it were to leave the gaily-coloured and noisy world and to wander into the wilderness; for what R. M. Rilke says in his Book of Hours is indeed true of God: “Thou art the miracle in the desert which strikes lost and lonely exiles.”
<|det|>text [62, 390, 888, 797]<|/det|>Let us remind ourselves once more of this: “that day” which spells freedom is also according to the word of Jesus Easter Day, the Day of Resurrection, the day which follows on the crucifixion. “You have sorrow now”—it is the sorrow of the passion, of the cross, by which all desires and hopes are destroyed. “But I will see you again and your hearts will rejoice.” The triumphant certitude of the resurrection is to be bestowed on the grief of the cross. And only he who is ready to enter with Jesus into the dread hour of Godforsakenness on the cross, will also be allowed to share in the joy of the Risen Saviour. To share in the cross of Christ, however, means to surrender our wishes and plans to the will of God, so that we place under the shadow of the cross all that concerns us, both our hopes and our work. This means that we enter the ultimate solitude of man before God, and, as Paul says, that we decree the sentence of death on ourselves, so that we put our trust not in ourselves but in God who raises the dead (2 Cor. 1: 9).
