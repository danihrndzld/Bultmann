<|det|>text [58, 333, 885, 560]<|/det|>Man can do many things. He can create great beauty in painting and sculpture, he can raise mighty buildings to embody his soaring fancies. And yet when he opens his being to the majesty of the mountains or the mysterious power of the sea, he may be swept by the sense of the pettiness of the artistic works which he creates in comparison with the over-awing splendour and sublimity of the works of nature. And considering the beauty of the flowers of the field he may grow deeply aware that even Solomon in all his glory was not arrayed like one of these (Matt. 6: 29).
<|det|>text [58, 561, 885, 789]<|/det|>Man can do many things. He can raise monuments of his skill which last for decades and even for centuries. And the greatest of his monuments retain an abiding worth for the successive generations. But they do not escape the law of transience and decay. Whole civilizations come to maturity and die, and nothing abides eternally. The work of man's hand passes away; but nature goes on its unvarying course throughout all ages, renewing its life with every spring and scattering lavishly its riches without ever becoming poorer.
