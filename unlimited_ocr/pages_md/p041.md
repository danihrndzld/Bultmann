We may certainly affirm that all decay and death are swallowed up in the great, heedless circling of her life, that every death in her midst only marks the transition to new life, and that in the total sphere of nature there prevails order, peace and intelligibility of pattern. <|det|>text [62, 239, 881, 556]<|/det|>Again, the worship of the powers of nature constantly runs the risk of being transformed into its opposite: into awe and terror of her unfathomable and sinister secrets. To many a questing mind it has seemed that the gay colours in which her laws bedeck themselves grew pale, and that these laws took on the appearance of an impassive, compulsive force: to them the order of the cosmos was imprisoning. No doubt Jesus can speak of the heavenly Father who causes His sun to shine on the evil and the good, and His rain to fall on the just and the unjust, but the poet who does not know this heavenly Father enthroned above nature sees the matter otherwise and says: "For unfeeling is nature: the sun shines on the evil and the good, and the moon and the stars illuminate the path of the evil-doer as of the upright."
<|det|>text [62, 558, 881, 738]<|/det|>At best there is operative in nature an uncanny, unfathomable power which can kindle no response in our heart. And Klopstock, who was not only a great German poet, but also a great Christian poet, addresses God present in nature thus: "Without Him who has loved me and sacrificed Himself for me, I could not be Thine! Without Him the thought of Thine all-pervading presence were terrible, an anguished fear of the all-powerful Unknown."
