Is such Christian existence, an existence in both pain and sorrow, an existence which is no longer rooted in this world, is it at all possible or is it merely a beautiful fantasy? Is not the promise of resurrection from the dead a perplexing and mysterious message for us?
<|det|>text [73, 226, 890, 560]<|/det|>Jesus anticipates such questions: “I have said this to you in figures” or more precisely translated “in enigmas”. For to him who does not dare to believe, the promise is a conundrum, a hollow sounding word. “The hour is coming when I shall no longer speak to you in figures, but tell you plainly of the Father.” This does not mean that later Jesus will say something new and different from what He has already said; it does mean that these puzzling words will seem transparently clear and true. This is shown straight-away by the remark of the disciples. Before Jesus says anything different from what He has already said, namely that He came from the Father and is returning to the Father, they remark: “Ah, now you are speaking plainly, and not in any figure!” The disciples speak thus from a faith which anticipates the fulfilment of the promise.
<|det|>text [68, 563, 888, 831]<|/det|>And what is Jesus' reply? "Do you now believe? The hour is coming, indeed it has come, when you will be scattered...." Hence He appears to attach but little importance to the confession of faith. But no, we should not understand it in that way! What we are to learn is rather this: a quick impulsive confession of faith may mean very little. Solid faith grows only in the experiences of life and especially in the experience of despair. And to this experience of despair the final word of His message applies: "I have said this to you, that in me you may have peace. In the world you have tribulation; but be of good cheer, I have overcome the world."
