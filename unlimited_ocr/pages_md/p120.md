<|det|>text [116, 72, 941, 476]<|/det|>When the Pharisee boasts of what he is and does, he is not lying. He is in truth a pious man distinguished for his piety and zeal, and in fact practises the works of piety which he enumerates. Nor is it his real failing that he forgets that there are also in his life, as in the lives of all men, things of which he could not boast before man and God. His real fault is rather that he should count up and boast of his good works in the presence of God at all; in supposing that by this means he can stand before God as “justified”, i.e. that God could not fail to deny him recognition and reward. With his piety he does in truth serve God; but he serves Him only in order to gain by this service recognition and reward. By this attitude he discloses that what fills his being to its depths is not love of God and goodness, but the need for recompense. The more he exacts from himself in the way of self-renunciation and sacrifice, the more passionate must be his need for reward; the more proudly he points to what he does, the keener is his sense of his own deserving.
<|det|>text [117, 479, 941, 884]<|/det|>But this means that the figure of the Pharisee does not belong simply to a past age, and is not exclusive to the Judaism of the period concerned or even to the Jewish race in general, but is characteristic of humanity as a whole. Modern psychology itself has taught us what a part is played in every human life by the impulse to self-assertion. How many of the words and deeds of men are attributable to the desire to gain authority and importance, words and deeds in respect of both good and ill, the moral and the immoral, great achievements and great crimes. Psychology has shown us how the natural need for recognition, if suppressed, can assume morbid developments, and how a human life may be ruined and brought to disaster if this desire for recognition is repressed so that it results in a so-called inferiority complex. For this reason great importance is attached in education to helping the child to realize that it can achieve something so that its consciousness of its own value may be strengthened, and it does not need to feel itself to be inferior.
