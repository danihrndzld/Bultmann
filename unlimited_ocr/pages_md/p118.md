<|det|>text [106, 143, 928, 725]<|/det|>But if we were to speak thus we should have misunderstood the story. We should not in any case have rightly understood it as a message addressed to ourselves. It is dangerous if it appears to us as something completely self-explanatory. We must not forget that when the story was narrated it seemed to the average listener at least to be obnoxious and offensive. They felt it as a defiant and offensive thing that the exemplary Pharisee, who represented a generally respected class of men, and who was irreproachable, should have been put to shame thus by being contrasted with the Publican, who belonged to a class that was despised and avoided, a class with which no self-respecting man could have anything to do. We need only consider what our reaction would be if we ourselves, who perhaps do not feel ourselves to be particularly distinguished examples of our profession and status, but are nevertheless proud that we do belong to a respected class and follow an honourable calling in life—if we were similarly put to shame by being contrasted with the example of a man who came from the most degraded and shunned sections of the community, from which only what was squalid and vulgar could be expected. In any case if the story does not strike us too as offensive and obnoxious, as it struck its first hearers; if we do not feel that we are perhaps intended by the Pharisee; then it is to be feared that we have not rightly understood it, and listened to it.
