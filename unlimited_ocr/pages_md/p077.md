The love of God which takes us not as those who we now are and into whom we have made ourselves, but as those who we are not, who we ought and wish to be, who we shall be. And by this means the divine love makes that which we are, apparent only, and bestows in its stead our genuine and true life.
<|det|>text [58, 184, 885, 386]<|/det|>This love of God is not a goal towards which we strive—who could ever obtain it by striving?—but it is the power which already enclasps and enfolds us, and to which our eyes have only to be opened; and we are to turn our gaze and our meditation on Him in whom it has appeared incarnate, Jesus Christ. To know oneself to be sustained by this love means to be free from the bondage of the past, from the fetters of oneself, free for the future which God will bestow and for the glory which shall be revealed to us.
<|det|>text [58, 388, 885, 728]<|/det|>This freedom creates in us the hope of which Paul says: "For in this hope we were saved. Now hope that is seen is not hope. For who hopes for what he sees?" Hope focuses itself on the invisible, on that of which we can have no conception or imagination. But this hope must be entertained in patience: "But if we hope for what we do not see, we wait for it with patience." This no doubt requires always new power and self-conquest. But if we are aware of the love of God we belong to those who have the "first fruits of the Spirit", i.e. those in whose present life the power of the future is effectually shown. And the Spirit "helps us in our weakness". When we feel our own powers enfeebled, then the power of God intervenes to help us, and our confession that in our own strength we can do nothing, becomes a prayer.
