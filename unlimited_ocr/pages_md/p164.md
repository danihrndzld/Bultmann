The novelty lies in Jesus' suggestion that the relation between God and man is the same as that between a master and his slave. This was a strange idea even for the Jewish audience of Jesus. Of course they were accustomed to speak of God as the almighty all-commanding Lord, who owes account to no man, but they regarded man as not being simply God's slave, but rather God's servant, rewarded by God and entitled to such a reward, and acquiring merit by the faithful fulfilment of his obligations. Hence according to Jesus this was false. The suggestion is not only strange and surprising, but offensive and vexaticus!
<|det|>text [108, 326, 936, 730]<|/det|>Still more is the suggestion offensive to the mind of modern man; and in this connexion I am thinking not only of the people of to-day but of man as his being and mode of thinking has developed during the past few centuries since the time of the Renaissance. He is of the opinion that in contrast with earlier and cruder epochs he cherishes a purified and spiritual idea of God: he thinks that God is not an arbitrary despot, a master in command of slaves, but as man himself is conscious of his own moral freedom and dignity, God too is for him the embodiment of ethical lofty ideals, a God who is honoured precisely by the fact that man in the dignity of his freedom can proudly raise his head to God. God is not sheer power, overwhelming, coercive, shattering, but rather spiritual power exercising sway over man through the inner life of the spirit, not humbling man but rather exalting him. Man would feel it to be demeaning to recognize himself as the slave of an arbitrary God, whose action cannot in any way be called to account.
