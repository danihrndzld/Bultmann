<|det|>text [92, 49, 923, 275]<|/det|>The remarkable thing is that Paul illustrates his thesis that our present existence is provisional and that our true and fulfilled existence is yet to come, by a consideration of the world of creatures outside the human sphere: “For the creation waits with eager longing for the revealing of the sons of God; for the creation was subjected to futility not of its own will but by the will of him who subjected it in hope; because the creation itself will be set free from its bondage to decay, and obtain the glorious liberty of the children of God.”
<|det|>text [96, 277, 923, 432]<|/det|>Even the creatures of the world of nature are not as they should be. They are filled with a longing for release from their bondage; against their own will, against what they truly should and want to be, they were subjected to futility, i.e. to transience. They cannot freely develop their forces of life; their freedom of growth is checked and they are maimed.
<|det|>text [96, 434, 923, 658]<|/det|>What is the meaning of this? Can we understand it? Have we not here a mythological mode of speech? Still more mythological is Paul's suggestion that nature was subjected to decay against its own will, and for the sake of Him who subjected it. In truth, Paul is here alluding to old mythological stories, elaborations of the old theme of Adam's fall: just as death was laid as a doom on man in consequence of his falling a victim to the seduction of Satan, so nature became doomed to the curse of decay and was thus involved in the tragic fate of man.
<|det|>text [96, 661, 927, 865]<|/det|>All this sounds very strange. For do we see the curse of decay at work in nature and do we hear its sighing for redemption? Does not nature seem to us on the contrary to be the sphere of inexhaustible vitality? In fact to such an extent is this the case that if we wish to picture plainly to ourselves what life is, we think straight away of the vital and creative forces ever operative in nature's life.
