<|det|>text [67, 157, 893, 496]<|/det|>The story is a parable. What instruction does it convey? One thing is quite clear: the rich man's invitation symbolizes the call of God which goes forth to invite men to enter His kingdom. The people invited—both those who were first invited as also those who were called in later—are the men to whom the call of God comes. And the hearers of the parable are asked whether it will be with them as with those who were first invited, who, because they did not hold themselves in readiness, missed the joy of the banquet. The tone of the parable is obviously not consoling and cheering. It is not that the teller is primarily thinking of those who were not originally invited and later were in fact summoned to share in the joy of the feast. The tone is rather solemn and threatening, as the conclusion plainly shows: "I tell you, none of those men who were invited shall taste my banquet."
<|det|>text [67, 497, 895, 744]<|/det|>It is easy to understand the parable in relation to Jesus and His time. The original guests are the pious members of the Jewish people, to whom Jesus' call to penitence was so often directed, and against whom His anger was so often kindled. These were men who for long had had special knowledge of God and His call; who for long had said "yes, yes!" and who now when Jesus comes with the proclamation that the time is fulfilled close their ears and harden their hearts against Him, and from whom He turns away to go to "tax collectors and sinners" who were willing to listen to His voice.
<|det|>text [71, 745, 895, 903]<|/det|>But if Jesus is speaking to us to-day through this parable, then He is summoning us also to consider whether we do not belong to the category of the original guests, who have long accepted God's call and God's invitation but who, when the matter becomes serious, calling for decision in the present moment, prefer to be absorbed in private affairs and to despise God's summons.
