<|det|>text [96, 40, 927, 472]<|/det|>A further indication of this is the present popularity of the stories of Adalbert Stifter with their descriptions of nature and of a type of life that fulfils its destiny in the midst of nature. And is not nature becoming increasingly absorbed in city culture and the technological organization of human life? Is it not being increasingly despoiled of its original peace and tranquillity and the mysterious sway of its inherent vitalities disturbed? It was still possible for Schiller to write: "There is perfection in the world of nature wherever man with his torment does not intrude." But where is it that man does not nowadays intrude? What does he not disturb with his tormenting unrest? What sphere is still left within the framework of our civilization, one might almost say on the whole earth, where in truth the peace and stillness and purity of nature reign undisturbed? The noise of modern traffic and the wail of modern jazz destroy the peace of even the remotest spots. Modern motor roads and the building of huge industrial plants utterly efface the natural beauties of our landscape.
<|det|>text [106, 472, 934, 899]<|/det|>It may perhaps be retorted that this is a romantic, aesthetic point of view. Is not this development inevitable? Is it not a question of harsh necessities which are rooted in the evolution of modern life? No doubt we are faced here to a large extent by sheer necessity. But should that blind us to the fact that nature which is thus integrated with the historical development and fate of man is being disfigured and defaced in the process? And does it all rest on sheer necessity? Where does a reasonable requirement begin and where does it end? Is not the ancient myth justified which suggests that the curse resting on nature has its origin in the sin of man? How much of this destructive process is the consequence not of the real exigencies of life, but of the demand for elaborate forms of pleasure, and the divorce of human life from nature?
