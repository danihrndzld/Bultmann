<|det|>text [62, 146, 885, 595]<|/det|>And this selfish envious spirit is too easily inclined to forget that it is impossible to establish complete equality among men, that there will always be diversities among them which are quite simply grounded in the diversity of human endowment, gifts, and powers, and that no human organizational technique is capable of eliminating the differences which fate decrees. But the mystery of our destiny lies in the hands of God. And we often make the mistake of requiring from God what we should require from ourselves as regards the structural arrangements of human society. This we do when we compare ourselves with others and feel discontented because they are faring better than ourselves. Such behaviour is the mean and envious behaviour of the labourers in the parable. What this parable requires of us is freedom from envy, and the capacity to rejoice in the good which others enjoy. In point of fact we cannot for the most part make a true judgment as to whether others are really better off than ourselves. We see the outward aspect of things and we often do not see how much inner sorrow and distress may be concealed beneath a fair outward show.
<|det|>text [62, 598, 885, 893]<|/det|>And finally we forget so very easily that all the craft and the effort involved in human organizing is in vain without the blessing of God: “Unless the Lord builds the house, those who build it labour in vain. Unless the Lord watches over the city, the watchman stays awake in vain” (Ps. 127: 1). Immersed as we are in our work and our effort to achieve things, we forget that God’s sovereign freedom also controls and disposes our human exertions and that in His freedom He can bring our work to prosperity, or lay it in ruins. And so long as we are faring well, we tend to ascribe our prosperity to our own skill and ability, and blind ourselves to God’s goodness which wills to encounter and confront us both in prosperity and adversity.
