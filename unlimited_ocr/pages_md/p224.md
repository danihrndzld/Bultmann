<|det|>text [120, 29, 951, 456]<|/det|>“Wait quietly”—this attitude however must be manifested in the quite concrete circumstances of our daily life, and precisely now in the crisis through which we are passing. We stand amid the ruins of a world that has collapsed, and we have to build it afresh. Not only is the outer structure of our world shattered—our cities and houses, our resources for trade and production, but the spiritual capital on which a people must live has also been swept away and annihilated: morals, the consciousness of right, and the rule of right itself. Everyone who is called to co-operate in some degree (and to a certain extent we are all so called) realizes what heavy work lies before us. It is work which brooks no delay; but amid all the energy required, and the urgent haste to put our hands to the plough, there is one thing of which we must again and again sharply remind ourselves. Patience is necessary! not patience in regard to our duties, but patience and inner calm regarding our expectations. Not patience as concerns what we have to demand of ourselves, but patience about what we demand of the future.
<|det|>text [120, 459, 945, 728]<|/det|>When here in Marburg a few weeks ago Professor Franz Bohm gave a lecture on the decline of law and right in the Third Reich, and in conclusion turned his thoughts to the future that is to emerge, he ended with the warning: "Have patience!" When in Whitsuntide week foreign experts met German professors to discuss together the crushing problems of international trade we had to listen to the repeated warning issued by foreign friends that we must exercise patience. Too much has happened, too much has been shattered and laid in ruins for our world to be put right again at one stroke and as if the terror through which we have passed had not taken place.
