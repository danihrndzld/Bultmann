<|det|>text [100, 238, 926, 463]<|/det|>But who is the man who can willingly and trustfully accept and open his heart to whatever may come to him? It is the man who can wholly surrender himself to God as His Creator, and resign himself to God's dealings; who can sincerely repeat the prayer of our Lord in Gethsemane: "Not as I will but as Thou wilt" (Matt. 26: 39). It is the man who knows himself to be the slave of God his Master. It is the man whom Jesus is referring to in this parable. In another parable the poet C. F. Meyer expresses the same attitude in the words which he puts into the mouth of Michaelangelo:
<|det|>text [99, 465, 926, 553]<|/det|>"I who am a slave of passion, make me in Thy image pure and free! The first man Thou madest out of clay, I will indeed be made of sterner stuff; forsooth, Master, now Thou needest Thy hammer! Strike, divine Sculptor, I am the stone."
<|det|>text [99, 554, 926, 780]<|/det|>Because the parable of Jesus invites us to surrender ourselves in this way, it is a word of grace, a word of liberation. Because it relentlessly smites from our hands our cherished rights and claims, it frees us to receive the grace of God. In the last analysis it has the same meaning as the word: "Truly I say to you, whoever does not receive the kingdom of God like a child, shall not enter it" (Mark. 10: 15). To be God's slave and to be His child is the same thing, and to those who have the child-like spirit, the promise runs: "To such belongs the kingdom of God" (v. 14).
<|det|>text [96, 781, 926, 895]<|/det|>But it is not easy to surrender our claimfulness! Is it not asking too much of our powers? Now the message of Jesus is finally shown to be a message of grace inasmuch as it drives us to our last place of refuge, to prayer, in order that we might receive strength to do what is required.
