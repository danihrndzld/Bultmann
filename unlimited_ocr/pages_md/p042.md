<|det|>text [96, 75, 920, 577]<|/det|>When man forgets this he violates his own dignity just as much as he violates the dignity of nature in equating his own structures with the powers and manifestations of nature. But if he violates the dignity of his humanity he also violates the honour of God who created man in His own image. And then he falls under the shadow of annihilating judgment. For if he forgets that he has to live his life in responsibility before God, his life becomes a dark mystery to him and his death a terror. He cannot of course truly understand his life in terms of the natural growth and development of plants and animals, for his life is always intertwined with responsibility and decision, temptation and guilt. Neither can he understand his death in terms of the withering and decay of vegetable life, as something utterly natural and self-explanatory. Rather to grow old and die is something that is repugnant and incomprehensible to him; it is fraught with terror. And though at all times loud voices have been raised in the endeavour to soothe and delude man's troubled spirit as it recoils in horror from his mysterious destiny, anguish breaks out in him ever afresh. For the man who knows God only in the realm of nature, death implies destruction, a destruction which makes his whole life meaningless.
<|det|>text [96, 579, 920, 898]<|/det|>Finally, a last word. If man goes no further than to be stirred by nature to feelings of devout gratitude, if he is not driven onwards to seek the face of the Creator, then his piety, however genuine, remains merely episodic and fragmentary. It remains as something which from time to time is awakened to life, which gives a religious tone to certain hours, which may indeed become vividly felt in the spring but which fades away again with the withering of the flowers. For the God whose presence man experiences in the brightness of nature, ceases to be felt in the grey light of common day, in the pressures of responsibility, in the storms of fate.
