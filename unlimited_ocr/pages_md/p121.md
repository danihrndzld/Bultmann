<|det|>text [43, 34, 857, 375]<|/det|>In fact, the need for recognition of one's value is something implanted in the very nature of man. We can only live and breathe freely in a circle where we are appreciated. We only feel ourselves to be uninhibited and secure when we have the feeling that we are esteemed at our full value. And just as we need that others should respect and appreciate us in our peculiar and characteristic worth, so we in our turn must respect the special merits of others and give them full recognition. But the decisive thing in this matter is that the recognition which we all need can never be made the real aim of our action and conduct. It can accrue to us only as the by-product of our behaviour, of our straightforward positive contribution, of our actual attitude towards our fellow men. Appreciation cannot be a goal to be striven after and attained with exertion and effort; it can only be given us as a gift.
<|det|>text [43, 376, 857, 758]<|/det|>The mistake made by so many is that they aim at appreciation as an end in itself, and they are anxious to attain it as a desirable good to the attainment of which they must concentrate their efforts. This urge for recognition can afflict a human life like a disease. It leads us astray into an impurity of motives, with the consequence that we do our work not disinterestedly for its own sake, but for our sake, and in order to gain credit for ourselves. A man who acts to increase his own prestige may well do great things; and it is just what we usually term record achievements which are often traceable merely to the urge for recognition. The greatness and true value of a work does not after all consist in the fact that it goes one better than other works of its kind, that it shows the intensity of the exertion and the extremity of the cost at which it has been achieved, but is decided simply by whether it is sensible and useful in its place, and serves the general good of the community.
