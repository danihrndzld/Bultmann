Ought they not rather to have rejoiced on hearing that their comrades, thanks to the kindness of their master, were to receive just as much as themselves so that they might have the wherewithal to sustain their own life and that of their families? Yes, these surly fellows could not but feel ashamed, and confess that the freedom of their master was exercised just in his generosity. And thus the parable is meant to teach us the unity of God's freedom and love.
<|det|>text [111, 276, 941, 771]<|/det|>Freedom and love belong integrally together in God and form a unity in His nature. No doubt we can imagine a freedom which manifests itself apart from love; a freedom of arbitrary and harsh power. But we cannot conceive any form of love and generosity, even amongst us men, existing without freedom. For we can only speak of a gift flowing from kindness when the gift is given not as a consequence of any external compulsion, not under the pressure of some so-called moral or social exigence, but indeed in perfect freedom, in conditions where the gift might not have been made at all, and where no claim on it exists. The activity of love can take place only where there is freedom. And were we to organize the whole complex of human relationships on a basis of right and law, to the exclusion of true freedom, so that everything which a man receives would rest on some just claim, then we should be banishing from the world the spirit of generous love, and so we should be excluding the very thing which imparts to the human soul real nobility and animates man with happiness and joy. Kindness is always undeserved. And what rejoices man's heart is precisely what he is given as a sheer gift, i.e. the bestowal of a gift which he has not deserved.
<|det|>text [111, 774, 941, 910]<|/det|>So it is too in man's relation with God. If we think of God only in the light of our claims, we shall never experience the goodness of God, for His goodness presupposes His freedom. And God's graciousness to man is always undeserved.
