Are not these obligations arising from our tasks in life and from our relations with our own, part and parcel of our very life? Ought we to seek release from them in order to gain divine freedom? At the present time should we repudiate our anxieties about our relations and friends who are fighting for us in dire distress and danger? Ought we to forget the sorrow that we bear in our hearts concerning those who have been taken from us? Our compassion with so many a one who is deeply afflicted? Would not the attainment of a freedom in which we are no longer moved by all this, amount to a subtle technique of withdrawing from the network of human relations, of retiring within oneself in a state of self-chosen isolation and seclusion, of rigid insensibility—we might even say inner deadness?
<|det|>text [118, 366, 943, 681]<|/det|>This cannot be what is meant by the words of Jesus. Otherwise He would not have been able to add that on “that day” we are still to pray and address our petitions to God. No! His promise concerns precisely those who stand involved in the complex relations of life, for in His high-priestly prayer He prays to God for His own: “I do not pray that Thou shouldst take them out of the world, but that Thou shouldst keep them from the evil one” (John 17: 15). It is not from the encounters of life that we should seek freedom but from ourselves; from the fact that in all these encounters we seek our own interests, holding fast to ourselves. Freedom from ourselves means a radical surrender of ourselves to God, and hence readiness for all that God sends us: openness to all the encounters of life.
