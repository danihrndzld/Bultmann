But such phenomena constitute neither miracle nor proof. They furnish no proof; for one might say (and it is in fact said) that Christianity had great significance for a certain epoch of history, but all that is over now and done with. Nor do they constitute the miraculous; for all spiritual and psychic forces work out their due effects in history, and everyone is influenced by them whether he is aware of it or not. But these things do not call for a decision nor are they offensive. Miracles, properly speaking, are only those events which essentially cannot be classified within natural and historical sequences, and which in the natural historical process stand out as arousing the sentiments of wonder, astonishment and repulsion. A miracle of such a kind is—quite simply—every straightforward manifestation of Christian love, which strikes us, shames us, convulses our being, and yet quickens us.
<|det|>text [96, 453, 923, 881]<|/det|>A miracle is in fact every deed—though it is not strictly speaking a human deed—and every event which takes place where the Spirit and mind of Christ hold sway. And where does Christ hold sway? Wherever the Word of the Gospel is preached and heard in faith. And for this reason the most important element in the answer of Jesus, and in the light of which alone the other affirmations are intelligible, is this: the poor have good news preached to them. For it cannot be denied that there are deeds which are inspired by nothing other than the proclamation of the Gospel of Christ; such deeds have arisen not from natural impulses, or from moral considerations, or from the intention of serving the common good and bettering human conditions, but simply and solely from love, the love which the Gospel of Christ awakens in the human heart. These deeds do not propose to change the world, nor are they done with a view to the effect which they will have on the world's life; they in fact happen because the Word of the Gospel has commanded them—nay, has liberated the spirit of man to perform them.
